## Introduction
---
QWERTY phones are making a comeback. Part of this is nostalgia for technology from the early 2000s. Including Blackberrys and Nokias. It seemed like the logical choice back then. If you were to have a computer in your pocket. It made sense to have a computer like keyboard. And a lot of people had gotten adept at sneaking away text messages without having to take their phone out in environments like the classroom. 

## What makes the idea appealing?
---
People love tactility. Feedback in non-visual media often helps us work faster. Freeing our eyes to focus on the things that matter. Power users often don't need to think of how they input things on to a computer. They don't need to look at the keyboard or the mouse. Their eyes are often affixed on the display constantly feeding on the feedback they get from their input. The idea that a phone can be as powerful is tempting.

## The Problems
---
### 1. QWERTY is not such a great layout

QWERTY is designed for typewriters to sell. I'm fairly convinced that the input from sales people influenced the design of QWERTY much more than the input from actual users (which isn't a problem in itself, they know what sells more than people know what to buy). The word TYPEWRITER can be typed on the top row alone. Then there are the practical constraints. Every key needed a physical input. It made sense to reduce them as much as possible. Moving the I and O (which could type 1 and 0) next to the numbers made sense. 

There are 26 letters and 8 fingers (leaving the thumb out). For most of your alphabet typing in english, you just need to assign every letter an average of 3.25 characters. So no matter how you arrange letters in three rows, someone is going to get good at it. That doesn't mean you can't optimise it. On a physical keyboard, you'd want the most used characters in the homerow. And the most used of them in the strongest fingers. On a physical keyboard, this has led to a number of alternative layouts in the years since. [[Dvorak]] is probably the most popular one in existence since the 1920s. [[Colemak]] is a recent favourite that recently gained first party support in all major operating systems. 

Physical keyboards in mobile phones did not stick around enough for alternative layouts to gain traction. Much of what drove the need for alternative layouts were people typing a lifetime in QWERTY developing finger injuries and pain. 

### 2. Typing characters is not how typing works

Most of typing involves typing "words". Fast typists, do not think of characters. The brain registers words to type and they're then input on to a keyboard like magic. That's a primary motivation behind all alternative layouts in existence. You have to think of letter combinations and make popular combinations easy to input. 

Physical keyboards on mobile phones just don't do that. You only have your thumbs to reach all 26+10 keys. One advantage is that the travel on a small keyboard isn't much. And your brain eventually adapts to the movement like touch and peak index finger typists do on a physical keyboard. However the limiting factor is the fact that you’re traversing over 30 characters with just two fingers. 

## Better ways to type on a phone
---

### 1. Swipe Typing

This lets you think of words when typing like in the physical keyboard world. Over time this becomes natural. 

### 2. Swipe typing with a better layout

QWERTY has problems with swipe typing too. Keeping I, U, and O together doesn’t work well when swiping. Common letters like bit, but and bot have very similar swipe patterns. An early attempt at solving swiping on a touch input device is [[ClearFlow Typing with Clarity and Flow|Clearflow]] (supported on both Gboard and FUTO keyboards on Android right now)

You’re still traversing over 30 characters on a small space. But the swipe paths are often much simpler than having to press individual keys.
